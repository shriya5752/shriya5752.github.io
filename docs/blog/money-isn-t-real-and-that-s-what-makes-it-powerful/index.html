Money Isn't Real (and that's what makes it powerful)

every currency in your wallet depends on the same invisible thing

imagine you’re stranded on an island with a suitcase containing ₹10 crore in cash.you can’t eat it. you can’t drink it. you can’t build a shelter with it. suddenly, the thing you’ve spent your entire life chasing becomes… paper.

so why does money have value?

the answer has almost nothing to do with the paper itself.

it has everything to do with one of the most powerful forces humans have ever created: shared belief

the question nobody asks

we grow up learning how to earn money, save money, invest money. nobody ever sits us down and asks the more basic question. why does this piece of paper lets me walk into a shop and walk out with food.

it’s not backed by anything you can touch. it’s backed by an agreement that everyone quietly signed without ever meeting each other.

that’s the part that gets me. money is probably the most successful piece of fiction humans have ever agreed to believe in. and once you see it that way, you can’t unsee it.

before money, there was a problem

go back far enough and humans didn’t have currency. they had barter. you had extra grain, i had a spare goat, we’d trade.

except barter has a brutal flaw called the double coincidence of wants. i need to want your goat at the exact moment you want my grain. if i’m a fisherman and you’re a shoemaker and you don’t need fish today, we’re stuck. i can’t exactly slice off a piece of my catch and tell you to come back next week for the rest.

so trade was slow, local, and fragile. it worked in small villages where everyone knew everyone. it completely broke down the moment human societies got bigger and more complex.

humans needed something that everyone would accept, regardless of what they personally wanted that day. something that acted like a placeholder for value.

then came trust, wearing different costumes

the first real solution wasn’t paper. it was trust, dressed up as something scarce.

shells, salt, cattle, and eventually metals like gold and silver became the placeholder. gold in particular won out for reasons that sound almost too simple: it doesn’t rot, it’s hard to fake, and there’s a limited amount of it. you couldn’t just walk into a field and grow more gold the way you could grow more wheat.

but here’s the thing people miss. gold itself is also kind of useless. you can’t eat it either. it has value because a huge number of people, across a huge number of cultures, independently agreed that it should. that agreement is the actual technology here, not the metal.

then paper money showed up, and at first, it wasn’t lying to you. early paper notes were literally receipts. you’d hand your gold to a goldsmith or a bank for safekeeping, and they’d give you a paper note promising you could redeem it for that exact gold whenever you wanted. the paper wasn’t the value. it was a claim on value sitting somewhere else.

the moment money stopped being a receipt

somewhere along the way, governments realized something interesting. as long as people trusted the system, most people would never actually show up demanding their gold back. so paper could be printed slightly ahead of the gold that technically backed it.

eventually, most countries dropped the gold backing entirely. this is called fiat currency, and it’s what almost every economy runs on today. fiat money has zero physical backing. no gold vault, no silver reserve. its entire value comes from one thing: the government says it’s legal tender, and enough people believe that will remain true tomorrow.

read that again. the rupee in your wallet has value because of collective, ongoing belief, not because of anything physically attached to it. the moment a country’s citizens stop trusting their currency, that currency can become worthless overnight, even if nothing about the paper itself has changed. this has actually happened, in places like Zimbabwe and Venezuela, where inflation spiraled so badly that people needed wheelbarrows of cash to buy bread.

Zimbabwe’s Inflation got so bad they printed $100 trillion bills

why bitcoin is the same idea, just without a government

this is where bitcoin gets interesting, and why i think it’s less of a “new kind of money” and more of an experiment in the oldest idea in economics.

bitcoin has no gold behind it. no government behind it. no physical form at all. its scarcity isn’t natural like gold, it’s mathematical, hardcoded so that only 21 million will ever exist. its trust isn’t backed by a central bank, it’s backed by a public, shared ledger that anyone can verify.

so bitcoin didn’t invent a new kind of value. it just found a new place to put the belief. instead of trusting a king, a goldsmith, or a government, you’re trusting code and a network of people all watching the same numbers.

same story, different costume.

why this is actually kind of beautiful

here’s what struck me when i really sat with this. every economic system humans have ever built, barter, gold, fiat, crypto, is really just a different technology for coordinating trust between strangers.

money lets a farmer in punjab and a software engineer in bangalore who will never meet, never speak the same amount, and never even know the other exists, still exchange value with each other seamlessly. that is an absurd level of coordination for a species that can’t even agree on where and what to eat.

and it only works because enough of us silently, collectively decided to keep believing in it.

the one question worth sitting with

so next time you look at your bank balance, or a stack of notes, ask yourself this.

what happens the day everyone stops believing at the same time?

it’s happened before, in hyperinflations and currency collapses. it’s not a hypothetical, it’s a historical pattern.

money isn’t valuable because of what it is. it’s valuable because of what we all agree to pretend, together, every single day.
